01 · where the rubric comes from

Two findings decide the format, and neither is a matter of taste

The rubric is not invented. It is two well-replicated results turned into checks a lint can run.

People do not read pages, they scan headlines

Nielsen Norman Group eyetracking, replicated for twenty years: readers get through 20–28% of the words, the average visit runs under a minute, and the dominant motion is the layer-cake — jump headline to headline, drop into body text only when a headline earns it.

Consequence: a topic-label heading like ## Architecture spends the one thing the reader reliably looks at, and returns nothing. Every heading has to carry a claim.

An assertion over a visual beats a phrase over bullets

Michael Alley's assertion-evidence work at Penn State, tested on 110 engineering students: a sentence assertion headline above explanatory graphics produced better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and stronger recall at delayed post-test than the phrase-headline-plus-bullet-list slide.

Consequence: headline states the claim, the visual is the evidence for that claim, and the text under it is one line — not a bullet pile restating the graphic.

Sources: NN/g — F-shaped pattern (original eyetracking) · How people read online (20–28% of words) · Penn State — How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension · Alley — assertion-evidence instruction set. Not used: transcript-rag-agent's corpus — it indexes YouTube transcripts on RAG engineering and holds nothing on presentation design, so searching it would have produced a citation that did not support the claim.

02 · the rubric

Seven dimensions, scored 0 / 1 / 2, and the first four outrank the last three

P1–P4 are the order and impact dimensions — what a scanner gets in the first screen, and whether the skill on display is legible. P5–P7 are hygiene. A page can be beautifully concise and still score badly, because concision is P5 and it is worth less than leading with the outcome.

The tie-break rule. When P1–P4 and P5–P7 pull against each other, P1–P4 wins. Adding a chart that pushes the page to 1,300 words is a good trade: P2 gains two points, P5 loses one. Cutting a caveat to save words is never a good trade — it costs P7 and it costs the argument.

# Presentation mode — how every page on this site presents information

Every published page is a presentation, not an essay. The unit is a **slide**:

    ASSERTION HEADLINE  — a claim, with its number
    VISUAL              — the chart, diagram or table that IS the evidence
    ONE INSIGHT LINE    — what it means, once, in under 25 words

Why: readers get through 20–28% of the words (NN/g eyetracking) and scan
headline-to-headline in a layer cake. A sentence assertion over a graphic
beats a phrase headline over bullets on comprehension, misconceptions,
cognitive load and delayed recall (Alley, Penn State, n=110).

## The rubric — score every page before publishing it

| Ref | Dimension          | Holds (2) when                                          |
|-----|--------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| P1  | Assertion headline | every heading states a claim, not a topic                |
| P2  | Visual evidence    | a chart/diagram/table under every claim                  |
| P3  | One insight        | one takeaway line per visual, <= 25 words                |
| P4  | Impact order       | outcome + skill in the first screen, setup last          |
| P5  | Scan cost          | <= 1,200 words, no paragraph over 60                     |
| P6  | Skill on display   | the AI-system capability is named and scored             |
| P7  | Honest number      | baseline / denominator / caveat adjacent to the number   |

**P1-P4 outrank P5-P7.** Adding a chart that costs words is a good trade.
Cutting a caveat to save words never is.

## Banned heading forms

`Architecture` · `Cost` · `What it is` · `Problem` · `The build` · `Overview`
`Setup` · `Results` — any noun that names a topic instead of asserting one.

Write `The model never touches the database` instead of `Architecture`.
Write `$0.004 an answer, and the cap is enforced in code` instead of `Cost`.

## Order, always

1. The outcome, with its baseline and its caveat.
2. The visual that proves it.
3. The mechanism — how it was made to happen.
4. The setup — what had to exist first.

Never the reverse. A reader who leaves after the first screen must still
have the result, and must know which engineering skill produced it.

## One insight, not a bullet pile

A bullet list under a chart that restates the chart is the AI-slop signature.
One line. If a second line is genuinely needed, the chart is doing too much —
split it.
